In what now feels like the distant past, I got my first PC around 2000, at 15: a modest K6-2 my father bought. At 16, I took a PC assembly course at FAETEC (a public technical education network in Rio de Janeiro). At 17, already in college studying languages and literature, I scraped together some cash and built my first PC from scratch, a Sempron, which let me fully live the much-missed, much-cursed years of dial-up, ICQ, MSN, IRC, Napster and so on. Later, around 2008, I used spare desktop parts to build my first home server, running the late Windows Home Server. When I wasn't reading Gabo (García Márquez) or Machado (de Assis), I was getting the hang of setting up and maintaining networks.

Fast forward to 2015: by then I was in my current job as a translator, and I got an HP ProLiant MicroServer Gen8. That's when playtime got serious. I remember that machine running OpenMediaVault, serving media files and running backups. Over the following years, I also tried Ubuntu and other Linux distros on the desktop; the server, besides Debian and Ubuntu, went through TrueNAS and Proxmox.

Around 2018, I moved to a bigger apartment and, since it needed renovating anyway, I planned a network from scratch, with Cat6 cabling running through the walls. From 2020 to 2023, I piled up networking gear aimed at the enterprise and prosumer markets, such as a JetStream gigabit managed switch, several TP-Link Omada EAP225 gigabit access points with the OC200 controller and an EdgeRouter X router (later replaced by a TP-Link TL-R605 to make my TP-Link fanboy card official), plus imported gear such as an NVIDIA Shield, a Sonos PLAY:1, Raspberry Pis and even an HDHomeRun, back when M2Play distributed it in Brazil. I also experimented with smart home stuff: Tuya-based switches, Sonoff relays, smart bulbs and the Echo Dot with Alexa.

During the pandemic, I started digging into the more technical side of self-hosting. I discovered a lovably nutty crowd that does this as a [hobby](https://www.reddit.com/r/selfhosted/) and became obsessed with the endless potential of Docker and of describing the whole infrastructure in Ansible. I also started managing a few VPSs (virtual private servers, in the cloud), hosting open source web services for storage, media and utilities, for family use and with a focus on convenience, privacy and control.

After the pandemic, with another move to a new apartment, I downsized. Today I have a more modest setup that's easier to maintain: modem, router and switch, a main server with 20 TB of storage and a backup server with 2× 2 TB for the most important data, both running Unraid, a TS Shara rack-mount sine wave UPS (2U, 1200 VA) and Raspberry Pis running AdGuard Home and NUT. I also run production services on VPSs from Oracle and Netcup. This whole infrastructure is [described as code with Ansible](https://github.com/lmagdiel/ansible-homelab): services in Docker containers, backups with restic and a disaster recovery routine, my own DNS, a VPN between the machines, a reverse proxy and monitoring.

In 2025, at 40, it became very hard to convince other people, and myself, that this 20-plus-year flirtation with IT was just a casual fling, so I decided to commit, in the form of a technology degree. I chose the [Internet Systems Technology](https://www.ead.senac.br/graduacao/tecnologia-em-sistemas-para-internet/) associate degree at Senac, which seemed to fit what I was doing and still wanted to do. Now in my second year, I can say it was a good call. My goal was, and still is, to gain more confidence to develop the interdisciplinary projects I care about, both personal and professional, at the deep intersection of IT with linguistics and translation, the most obvious example of which is LLMs. It's no accident that, as my [projects](/en/tecnologia/#projects) show, I've been applying technology to everything in my main field: from literature and journalism, traditionally more "analog," to heavily technology-driven fields such as technical translation and subtitling.
